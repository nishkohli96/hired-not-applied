# Value method

## 1. Career patterns

Build a quick matrix: for each role/project, note **problem faced → what the user did → outcome**. Then look across rows for repeats:
- **Problems frequently solved** (e.g. slow systems, messy launches, failing integrations, low conversion, scaling a team).
- **Outcomes consistently delivered** (faster, cheaper, more revenue, more users, fewer incidents, shipped on a hard deadline).
- **Challenges often tackled** (greenfield builds, rescues, migrations, high-traffic events, regulated work).
- **Situations where the user adds most value** (early-stage chaos, scale-up, big-brand client delivery, turnaround).
- **Types of work they excel at** (building platforms, leading delivery, improving performance, reusable tooling).

A pattern needs 2+ independent data points. Say how many support each.

## 2. Strengths (five lenses)

| Lens | Look for |
|---|---|
| Technical | Tools, systems, depth, craft artefacts (open source, certifications) |
| Functional | Domain know-how: payments, media/OTT, e-commerce search, healthcare ops, etc. |
| Leadership | Team size led, mentoring outcomes, ownership, decisions made, cross-team influence |
| Commercial | Revenue, conversion, cost, client wins, brand-name delivery |
| Strategic | Choosing what to build, architecture direction, reusable assets, long-term bets |

For each strength: **evidence** (role + fact), **why it matters** to employers now, **how it creates value** (the mechanism, not the adjective).

## 3. Value creation types

Rank the user's strongest from: revenue growth, cost reduction, operational improvement, team performance, customer outcomes, business transformation, risk reduction, innovation, process improvement, market expansion, speed to market, reliability/quality. Rank by number of supporting outcomes, then by size of the numbers.

## 4. Business impact themes (3–5)

Name each theme in plain words a hiring manager would repeat (e.g. "Makes high-traffic products faster", "Turns client deadlines into shipped releases", "Builds tools other engineers adopt"). Avoid stock phrases unless the evidence is strong and specific. Each theme: 2+ pieces of evidence.

## 5. Leadership identity

From observable evidence only (team size, mentoring outcomes, awards, promotions, repeated ownership, reviews/testimonials if provided):
- What people likely rely on the user for.
- What colleagues likely associate them with.
- What managers likely value most.
- What teams likely appreciate about their leadership.
Mark each with its basis. If there's no people-leadership evidence, frame leadership as ownership and say so.

## 6. Unique value proposition

- Business problems they're best equipped to solve.
- Situations where they create exceptional value.
- Organisations most likely to benefit (stage, size, industry, situation, e.g. "post-Series B product companies scaling a web platform").
- Roles where they're most valuable (titles and level).
"Unique" = the **combination** that few others have (e.g. deep stack + domain + leadership + a public proof like open source), not a single superlative.

## 7. Statement formulas

- **One-sentence**: `I help {organisation type} {achieve outcome} by {how, distinctive}, as I've done at {proof}.` (≤30 words, or a tighter variant without "I help" if it reads better.)
- **Executive**: who you are (identity + years) → the problem you're known for solving → 2 proof points with numbers/brands → the leadership pattern → what you want to do next.
- **Recruiter**: title-level identity, years, core stack/domain → 2 proof points → leadership scope → availability/location if in the resume. Plain, scannable, keyword-rich.
- **LinkedIn headline**: `{Role} | {what you do, outcome-led} | {proof or niche}`, ≤220 characters. **About opener**: first person, the pattern, one story-led proof, what you're looking for.

All four must be consistent: same core claim, different length and audience.

## 8. Evidence check

For every major claim in the themes, UVP and statements:
- **Strong**: a number, a named brand/client, an award, a third-party quote, or repeated across 2+ roles.
- **Moderate**: clear responsibility with partial or qualitative outcome, or a single instance.
- **Weak**: self-description without an outcome, or inferred from title only.
Weak claims don't go into statements. Say what evidence would upgrade each.

## 9. Blind spots (executive-coach view)

- **Undervalued strengths**: things buried in the resume that are rare or in demand (e.g. open-source adoption, high-traffic event delivery, test-quality culture, AI-assisted delivery).
- **Overlooked achievements**: outcomes mentioned without emphasis or numbers.
- **Underrated experience**: brand names, regulated domains, crisis delivery, cross-border teams.
- **Positioning gaps**: where the current resume framing undersells (e.g. leadership hidden under an IC title, outcomes stated as activities).
Challenge weak conclusions: if the evidence doesn't support a flattering theme, say so.

## 10. Final value assessment

Four short answers: strengths that appear across multiple roles · leadership qualities people rely on · outcomes consistently created · business problems repeatedly solved.
