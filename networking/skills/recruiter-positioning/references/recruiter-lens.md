# Recruiter lens

## How recruiters actually work

- Targets: placements per quarter, time-to-submit, submittal-to-interview ratio. Every candidate they submit spends their credibility with the hiring manager.
- First screen: 6–30 seconds on title, current company, years, location, headline, top 2–3 bullets.
- They search with boolean strings on job titles, core skills, industry terms and locations; profiles without those exact words don't appear.
- They "sell" a candidate internally in 2–3 sentences. The user's job is to write those sentences for them.
- Low risk to them = clear level, clear direction, stable story, availability and logistics known, consistent resume and LinkedIn, numbers they can repeat.

## 1. Positioning formulas

- **Placement line**: `<seniority> <function> for <company type/team>, strongest in <A> and <B>.`
- **50-word recruiter intro**: identity (level + function + years) → 2 proof points (numbers, brands) → leadership scope → target role + location/availability.
- **100-word executive summary**: identity → the problem they're known for solving → 3 proof points (one leadership, one impact, one scale/brand) → domain → what they want next and where.
- **What I do**: one sentence, verb-led, outcome-led ("I build and lead delivery of <what> that <outcome>").
- **Headline** (LinkedIn ≤220 chars): `<Target title> | <core stack/function> | <domain> | <one proof>`. Use the target title recruiters search for, as long as the user's experience supports it.

## 2. Impact themes recruiters notice

Prioritise: brand names, scale (users, traffic, revenue, team size), measurable outcomes (%, time, money), leadership scope, and anything that matches the target role's top requirement. Rank achievements by "would a recruiter repeat this to a hiring manager?".

## 3. Marketability and risk checklist

| Strength recruiters like | Check |
|---|---|
| Easy to understand | One clear function and level; title progression makes sense |
| Easy to pitch | 2–3 repeatable proof points with numbers |
| Commercially attractive | Outcomes tied to revenue, cost, users, reliability |
| Lower risk | Stable tenure pattern or a clear reason for moves; consistent dates; availability stated |
| Memorable | One distinctive asset (big brand, open source, rare domain combo) |

| Concern | Typical trigger | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Positioning confusion | Headline says one thing, recent roles another | Align headline, summary and target title |
| Excessive breadth | Long skills list, "full-stack/DevOps/mobile/data" | Lead with the 5–8 skills the target role needs |
| Unclear direction | Mixed target titles, IC vs manager ambiguity | Pick one primary target; name the secondary |
| Weak impact | Duties not outcomes | Rewrite top bullets as outcomes with numbers |
| Overcomplicated messaging | Project names without context, acronyms | Say what it is, for whom, and the result |
| Inconsistent narrative | Title/date mismatch resume vs LinkedIn, gaps unexplained | Make them match; one-line reason for gaps/short stints |
| Role misalignment | Target title above or beside proven scope | Use the supported title; show the stretch through proof |

Short tenures or layoffs: one neutral line ("role ended in a company-wide layoff") prepared, not volunteered first.

## 4. Search optimization

- **Core title keywords**: the target title plus 2–3 variants recruiters use (e.g. Senior Software Engineer, Senior Full Stack Engineer, Tech Lead, Lead Engineer).
- **Functional cluster**: the core stack/skills, spelled the way JDs spell them (and acronym + full form once).
- **Leadership cluster**: "team lead", "mentoring", "code reviews", "led a team of N", "technical leadership", "stakeholder management" — only if supported.
- **Industry phrases**: domain nouns (OTT, streaming, e-commerce, fintech, payments, SaaS, B2B).
- **LinkedIn fields**: headline, About first 3 lines, current title, Skills (top 3 pinned), Open to Work job titles and locations, Featured.
- **Sourcing strings**: 2–3 sample boolean strings a recruiter might use to find the user, so the user can test their own visibility.

## 5. Recruiter-call strategy

- Open with the placement line, then let the recruiter lead.
- Reinforce the same 2–3 themes every time; recruiters repeat what they hear consistently.
- Answer logistics cleanly and early: notice period/availability, location/work mode, work authorization if relevant, compensation approach (a range or "open based on scope", per the user's choice).
- Confidence signals: concise answers, numbers, clear reasons for moves, knowing what they want, responsiveness, a ready resume tailored to the role.
- Simplify: project names without context, deep technical detail, long career narratives, multiple target directions.
- Doubt creators: vague reasons for leaving, badmouthing employers, "open to anything", hesitancy on availability, titles that don't match LinkedIn.

## 6. Weak → stronger phrases (examples to adapt)

| Avoid | Why it hurts | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Responsible for…" / "Worked on…" | Activity, no outcome | "Cut / built / led … resulting in <number>" |
| "Passionate, hard-working team player" | Generic, unverifiable | One proof point with a number |
| "Open to any role" | No placement answer | "Targeting <title> in <location/mode>" |
| "I was kind of leading the team" | Undercuts seniority | "I led a team of <N> engineers" (if true) |
| "Full stack, DevOps, mobile, data, AI…" | Breadth confusion | "Full stack, strongest in <A> and <B>" |
| "I'm a tech lead" (without the title or scope) | Credibility risk if checked | "Senior engineer leading a team of <N>" |
| "Long story, but…" | Signals a messy narrative | One-line reason, then move on |
